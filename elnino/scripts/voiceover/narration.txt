## 1
Right now, in the middle of the Pacific Ocean, a patch of water is heating up to levels never recorded before. It's thousands of kilometres from any major city. And yet, over the next few months, it will help decide whether Australia burns, whether Peru floods, and how much you pay for your morning coffee. Its name is El Niño. And this one could be one of the strongest ever measured.
## 2
To understand El Niño, start with a normal year. Along the equator, steady winds called the trade winds blow from east to west, from South America toward Asia. They push warm surface water across the Pacific, so it piles up near Indonesia and Australia. There, the sea is actually about half a metre higher than off Peru. Meanwhile, off South America, cold water rises from the deep to replace it. Warm in the west, cold in the east. That's the Pacific's default setting.
## 3
Now, every few years, something breaks. The trade winds weaken. Sometimes they even reverse. And all that warm water piled up in the west starts sloshing back east, like water in a bathtub. The cold water off Peru stops rising. The eastern Pacific heats up. This matters because warm ocean means more evaporation, more clouds, more rain. So when the warm water moves, the rain moves with it. The storms that normally soak Indonesia drift toward the middle of the Pacific and South America. And because the atmosphere is connected, this shift ripples across the planet, bending jet streams and shifting monsoons thousands of kilometres away. Fishermen in Peru noticed it centuries ago: warm water arriving around Christmas. They called it El Niño, the child, after the baby Jesus.
## 4
Scientists track El Niño by watching one box of ocean in the central Pacific, called Niño three point four. When it stays more than half a degree warmer than usual for several months, El Niño is declared. This year, it's not half a degree. The World Meteorological Organization expects that box to average around three point seven degrees warmer than normal from October to December, with a peak around December. It says El Niño is firmly established and strengthening rapidly, and gives it a near one hundred percent chance of lasting through February twenty twenty seven. Put it next to the giants of the past: nineteen eighty two, nineteen ninety seven, twenty fifteen. This one is in the same league, or beyond.
## 5
So what happens on the ground? El Niño doesn't hit everyone the same way. It reshuffles the weather. In Indonesia, the Philippines and Australia, rain tends to disappear. Drought brings crop losses and raises the risk of massive wildfires. Southern Africa often faces failed harvests. India's monsoon can weaken. In northern Brazil, the Amazon can dry out. Meanwhile, other places get too much water. The coasts of Peru and Ecuador can see torrential rain and deadly floods in normally dry deserts. Southern Brazil, Uruguay and northern Argentina, the southern United States, parts of East Africa: all tend to get wetter. And almost everywhere, the dice are loaded toward heat. The World Meteorological Organization forecasts above-average temperatures across large parts of the globe through the end of the year. Same ocean, opposite disasters.
## 6
You don't have to live in these places to feel it. El Niño is an economic event. Cocoa, coffee, palm oil, rice and sugar are grown in regions it hits hardest, so droughts there show up as higher prices in supermarkets everywhere. Off Peru, the warm water drives away the anchovies that feed one of the world's biggest fisheries, which affects fishmeal and fish farming worldwide. Past strong El Niños have cost the global economy trillions of dollars over the following years.
## 7
There's one more twist. El Niño doesn't add new heat to the planet. But it moves heat from the ocean into the air. And this time, it arrives on top of decades of global warming. That's why the years following strong El Niños tend to break global temperature records. Twenty sixteen did. Twenty twenty four did. Many scientists expect twenty twenty seven could be next.
## 8
El Niño is natural. It's been happening for thousands of years. What's new is the world it's landing in. Over the next few months, a patch of ocean most people will never see will shape their weather, their food, and their year. Now you know where to look.
